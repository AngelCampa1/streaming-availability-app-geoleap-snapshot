# Why streaming content varies by country — and the business models powering it

Streaming libraries differ dramatically across borders because the entertainment industry still operates on a territorial licensing system designed for a pre-internet era, where rights to films and shows are sold country by country to the highest bidder. Netflix's catalog varies as much as **40–60% between countries** not because of a technical limitation but because a web of pre-existing broadcast deals, co-production agreements, cultural regulations, and strategic pricing decisions fragments content across platforms and borders. This guide covers the complete mechanics — from how a single film generates revenue across seven distribution windows to why Netflix charges $1.72/month in India and $24.99 in the United States — with real deal values, ARPU figures, and case studies drawn from public filings and trade reporting.

The streaming industry generated over **$180 billion globally in 2025**, split across subscription (SVOD), ad-supported (AVOD), transactional (TVOD), and free ad-supported streaming TV (FAST) models. Yet despite its scale, the business remains governed by licensing structures that predate streaming itself. Understanding why content appears in one country and not another requires unpacking territorial rights mechanics, windowing economics, co-production agreements, and the platform strategies that determine what viewers see, when they see it, and how much they pay.

## How territorial licensing deals actually work

The global entertainment industry sells distribution rights territory by territory — a system that originated in the theatrical era and persists today. At film markets like Cannes, the American Film Market, and Berlin's European Film Market, sales agents represent producers and negotiate deals for individual countries or regions. Major studios like Sony, Universal, and Warner Bros. negotiate directly with platforms for output deals covering entire slates, while independent distributors handle smaller titles through aggregators and regional buyers.

Three primary deal structures dominate the market. **Flat-fee deals** involve a one-time payment for specific territorial rights over a defined period — Netflix prefers this model for cost predictability. **Revenue-share arrangements**, common on AVOD platforms, split income 60/40 or 70/30 between licensor and platform. **Minimum guarantee plus overage deals** combine an upfront payment with performance-based royalties, typically used for high-value content with uncertain demand.

For Netflix Originals, the dominant structure is the **cost-plus model**: Netflix pays 100% of production costs plus a **~30% overhead premium** in exchange for global rights, often held exclusively for up to 10 years. Producers receive no backend participation — no share of downstream revenue from merchandising, syndication, or international sales. This model proved transformative when it launched with *House of Cards* in 2012, but it has drawn criticism from creators who forgo potentially massive upside.

### What studios charge — and what platforms pay

The economics of territorial licensing span an enormous range. For an independent or catalog film in a mid-sized European territory, a flat SVOD license might run **$5,000–$50,000**. A mid-tier title in a major territory like the US or UK typically commands **$500,000–$5 million**, while premium blockbuster content can reach **$5–$50 million per territory**. Global output deals involving entire studio slates run into the billions.

Netflix paid roughly the same amount ($500M+) for **global** Seinfeld rights as NBCUniversal allocated for **US-only** Office rights. The difference: Netflix had 150+ million global subscribers to amortize the cost across, making global deals economically viable at similar headline figures. Typical SVOD license windows run **18 months to 5 years**, with mega-deals extending to 7 years. Netflix amortizes content licensing costs over approximately 10 years on its balance sheet.

## Netflix's library varies far more than most subscribers realize

As of late 2025, Netflix's global catalog ranges from roughly **900 titles in Sudan to over 9,700 titles in Iceland** — a staggering 10x variation. Among major markets, the United Kingdom leads with approximately **8,893 titles**, followed by Australia (8,073), the United States (7,865), and Canada (7,846). The UK actually surpassed the US by over 1,000 titles in 2025, partly driven by EU content quota requirements mandating at least **30% European works** on streaming platforms operating in Europe.

Licensed movies drive the biggest variation between territories. When only TV shows are counted, Slovakia — which tops overall catalog rankings — drops from first to twenty-second globally. Netflix Originals provide the most consistent global availability, since content Netflix produces is "generally made available to its users around the world." However, even Originals have exceptions: *Cunk on Earth* is branded a Netflix Original in the US but not the UK, while *Better Call Saul* carries the Original label in the UK but not domestically.

The reasons for this fragmentation are structural. Before Netflix entered most international markets, studios had already sold TV, cable, and sometimes SVOD rights to local broadcasters. In Japan, a title might be locked into a deal with a local pay-TV provider. In France, Canal+ holds premium first-window rights through the country's legally mandated *chronologie des médias*. Even when Netflix wants to acquire content for a specific territory, it may face situations where no one is selling rights in that region, the title is exclusive to a competitor, or multiple rights holders complicate negotiations.

### The originals strategy was a direct response to licensing fragmentation

Netflix's aggressive pivot to original content — now **50%+ of its US library** and growing — was partly a strategic response to territorial licensing complexity. Owning the underlying IP means controlling global distribution, merchandising, and sequels in perpetuity, eliminating the need for complex, expiring territorial negotiations. When Disney, WarnerMedia, and NBCUniversal launched competing platforms and pulled their content (Friends, The Office, Marvel films, Pixar content), Netflix's investment in originals proved prescient as insulation against this risk.

Netflix added **597 new Originals in 2025**, up from 589 in 2024 and 568 in 2023. The company committed multi-billion-Euro investments in Spanish programming through 2028, **$2.5 billion for South Korean content**, and $1 billion for Mexican productions. Yet the pendulum has partially swung back: studios are now re-licensing content to Netflix (Warner Bros. licensed *Sex and the City* and *Band of Brothers*; *Suits* exploded on Netflix after leaving Peacock). Notably, since January 2020, not a single Netflix original movie has ranked among the **50 most in-demand films worldwide**, according to Parrot Analytics — licensed content still dominates film engagement despite being less than half of titles.

## The windowing system that governs every release

Every major film passes through a sequential chain of distribution windows, each offering diminishing exclusivity at declining price points. The traditional sequence runs: **theatrical → PVOD/EST → TVOD → Pay-1 SVOD → Pay-2 SVOD → premium cable → basic cable/free TV → FAST/AVOD**. Each window generates revenue for the studio, but the economics and timing have shifted dramatically.

The theatrical-to-digital window has compressed from a standard **90 days in 2019 to an average of just 32 days by 2024**. Individual studios vary significantly: Universal averages just 20 days (with a deal giving AMC theaters 17-day exclusivity for films opening under $50M and 31 days for bigger releases), while Disney maintains the longest windows at 58 days. Sony holds steady at roughly 45 days. Warner Bros. settled into metronomic 77-day theatrical-to-streaming windows for its 2025 slate after its controversial 2021 experiment of releasing its entire theatrical calendar day-and-date on HBO Max.

The revenue dynamics at each stage explain why studios guard windows carefully. Studios retain approximately **50% of domestic theatrical gross** (less internationally, often 40–45%), but they keep roughly **80% of PVOD revenue**. This economic advantage made PVOD a studio favorite during the pandemic: Universal's *Trolls World Tour* generated **$100 million in PVOD revenue** in its first three weeks at $19.99 per rental, with Universal netting approximately $77 million — superior to what a modest theatrical run might have returned after exhibitor splits.

### France's legally mandated windows are the global outlier

France uniquely regulates distribution windows by law through its *chronologie des médias*. Under the 2022 agreement, Netflix must wait **15 months after theatrical release** to stream French cinema on its platform — down from a previous 36 months but still vastly longer than any other market. Disney+ and Amazon must wait 17 months. Canal+, France's dominant pay-TV provider and the largest single investor in French cinema at **€160–190 million annually**, gets access at just 6 months. Netflix challenged this system before the Conseil d'État in 2025, seeking a 12-month window, while Disney+ secured a reduced 9-month window by agreeing to invest 25% of its French revenue in local production.

### Co-production deals create territorial patchworks

When Netflix or Amazon co-produces with a local broadcaster, the resulting rights split creates permanent territorial gaps. The typical structure gives the streamer rest-of-world streaming rights in exchange for partial funding, while the broadcaster retains domestic broadcast and catch-up rights. According to Ampere Analysis, **56% of upcoming Netflix and Amazon Originals** from the UK, Spain, Denmark, and the Netherlands are co-productions with local broadcasters, with broadcasters typically bearing about **75% of production costs** while the streamer acquires near-global rights at a discounted rate.

The *Call My Agent!* franchise illustrates this complexity perfectly. France Télévisions held French broadcast rights; Netflix acquired global streaming rights. But the show spawned local remakes across territories — an Indian remake on Netflix, a British version on Prime Video, Italian on Sky, German on Disney+ — creating an entirely separate rights patchwork layered on top of the original. When BBC co-produces with Netflix (as with *Dracula* or *Troy: Fall of a City*), the BBC retains exclusive UK rights on iPlayer, meaning Netflix cannot stream that content in Britain despite having funded it. BBC Director General Tony Hall signaled in 2019 that the BBC would do "much less" co-production with streamers going forward, recognizing that ceding international rights was "mortgaging your future."

## Every streaming business model currently in use

The streaming ecosystem now encompasses six distinct business models, each with different revenue mechanics, pricing logic, and per-user economics. Most major platforms operate hybrids.

### SVOD generates the highest per-user revenue

Subscription video on demand remains the industry's revenue engine. Netflix reported **$39 billion in 2024 revenue** across 302 million global subscribers, with US & Canada ARPU of **$17.26/month** (Q4 2024) — the highest in the industry. By contrast, EMEA ARPU was $11.11, Latin America $8.00, and Asia-Pacific just $7.34. Netflix stopped disclosing subscriber counts and ARPU starting Q1 2025, projecting $43–44 billion in 2025 revenue.

Disney+ domestic ARPU reached **$8.06–$8.09/month** by mid-2025, while Hulu's SVOD ARPU stood at $12.36 and its Live TV tier commanded a remarkable $99.94. Warner Bros. Discovery's Max reported domestic ARPU of **$10.40–$11.77** but international ARPU of only $3.63–$3.85, reflecting the challenge of scaling internationally at premium prices. Paramount+ reported global ARPU of $7.80 with 79 million subscribers. The combined Disney+/Hulu operation achieved **$1.33 billion in operating profit** in FY2025, a dramatic turnaround from just $143 million the prior year.

### AVOD and FAST are growing fast but monetize far less per user

Ad-supported models generate a fraction of SVOD revenue per viewer. Tubi, Fox's free streaming platform, reached **over 100 million monthly active users** and approximately $1 billion in annual revenue by 2025, implying an estimated ARPU of roughly **$1.30/month**. Pluto TV (Paramount) reported similar per-user economics at about $1.38/month with 80+ million MAUs. Samsung TV Plus has emerged as a major FAST player with **88 million MAUs** and nearly 700 channels in the US alone.

The FAST market generated **$4.9 billion in US revenue in 2024** and is projected to reach $9 billion by 2029. FAST channels operate on a revenue-share model where platforms split ad revenue with content providers, typically **45/55 to 50/50**, with CPM rates ranging from $8–$25 depending on genre, platform, and territory. US CPMs run **3–5x higher** than European FAST CPMs, which is why studios pursue US-first FAST deals. Ad fill rates remain a challenge at only **20–40%** across the industry. A catalog title driving 500,000 monthly views at $12 CPM on a 50/50 revenue share generates roughly $3,000/month — meaningful at scale across dozens of titles, but a different universe from SVOD licensing economics.

### PVOD carved out a permanent niche during the pandemic

Premium video on demand — early-access digital rentals at **$19.99–$29.99** — became a permanent feature of the distribution landscape after *Trolls World Tour* proved the model in 2020. Studios keep approximately 80% of PVOD revenue versus roughly 50% of theatrical, making it economically attractive. *Black Widow* generated an estimated **$60 million through Disney+ Premier Access** at $29.99 alongside $158 million in theatrical revenue. The TVOD market (standard digital rentals at $3.99–$6.99 and purchases at $14.99–$19.99) totaled approximately **$4 billion in US revenue in 2024** — essentially flat with 2014 after adjusting for inflation — with Amazon controlling roughly 50% market share.

### The ad tier changed hybrid economics permanently

Netflix's ad-supported tier reached **94 million monthly active users** by mid-2025, with over 55% of new signups in ad-available markets choosing the cheaper option. Netflix's average ad CPM of approximately **$37** in 2024 (declining toward $31 in 2025) exceeded competitors: Amazon Prime Video launched at ~$24, Hulu ranged from $10–$30, and Peacock charged $15–$35 programmatically. Netflix's ad revenue roughly doubled in 2024 to an estimated $2.15 billion and was on track to double again to approximately **$3 billion in 2025**, with a long-term target of $9 billion by 2028–2030. Amazon's entry into ad-supported streaming in January 2024 — defaulting all 115 million US Prime Video viewers into an ad tier — added approximately **50 billion ad impressions** to the US connected TV market, creating significant downward CPM pressure across the industry. Total US CTV ad spending reached an estimated $32.6 billion in 2025.

## Streaming is rebundling — and the data proves it works

After a decade of unbundling from cable, the streaming industry is reconverging toward bundles at remarkable speed. The pattern follows what analysts call the classic "bundle → unbundle → rebundle" rhythm of commerce. American households now subscribe to an average of **3.9–4 paid streaming services**, spending a collective **$69/month on streaming in 2025** — up 44% from $48 just two years earlier. Sixty-two percent of subscribers report "subscription fatigue," up from 46% in 2020, and **42% regularly cancel and resubscribe** in a pattern the industry calls "churn and return."

The data on bundle effectiveness is unambiguous. The Disney+/Hulu/Max cross-company bundle, launched in July 2024 at $16.99/month with ads, achieved an **80% three-month retention rate** — outperforming Netflix (74%), Disney's own internal bundle (73%), standalone Hulu (56%), and standalone Max (55%). Disney bundle subscribers are **59% less likely to churn** within 12 months compared to standalone Disney+ subscribers. Industry-wide, bundling reduces churn by approximately **34%**, according to Bango. When monthly churn across premium SVOD averaged 5.5% in early 2024 — meaning platforms collectively had to sign up **142 million gross subscribers** in 2023 just to net 24 million — the economic case for bundles became irresistible.

Charter's Spectrum has taken the most aggressive approach, bundling ad-supported versions of Max, ESPN Unlimited, Hulu, Disney+, Paramount+, Peacock, AMC+, and several other services into its TV packages — a retail streaming value exceeding **$100/month** — as part of cable subscriptions starting at $95–$115. The strategy is working: Charter's TV customer losses dropped to 181,000 in Q1 2025 from 405,000 a year earlier. Carriers have followed suit: Verizon offers Netflix + Max with ads for $10/month, while T-Mobile includes Netflix Standard with ads free on premium plans.

### Whether this recreates cable's problems depends on price discipline

The parallels to cable are real. Streaming services raised prices by an average of **12% in 2024**, with many hiking again in 2025. The Disney+/Hulu/Max bundle increased $3/month within its first year to $19.99 with ads. Comcast's StreamSaver went from $15 to $18 in 18 months. At $69/month for streaming plus $50–$70 for internet, cord-cutters now spend $120–$140 monthly — approaching cable-era costs of $125. But critical differences persist: consumers can cancel streaming services instantly with no contracts, ad-supported tiers provide genuine low-cost entry points ($7–$11 each), and bundles combine 2–3 curated services rather than 200 channels. The question is whether annual price increases will erode these advantages. BCG has modeled that a comprehensive **80-million-subscriber general entertainment bundle** priced at roughly $50/month could achieve mid-teens profit margins while dramatically reducing the industry's churn problem.

## Regional pricing reveals a 19x gap driven by local economics

Netflix's most expensive plan costs **$30.56/month in Switzerland** while its cheapest runs **$1.61/month in Pakistan** — a 19x spread that reflects deliberate purchasing-power-parity adjustments rather than arbitrary pricing. The United States sits near the top at $24.99 for Premium, while major emerging markets offer dramatically lower entry points: India's mobile-only plan at ₹149 ($1.72), Nigeria's basic tier at ₦3,500 ($2.33), Egypt at EGP 100 ($1.98), and Brazil at R$20.90 ($3.63).

The revenue implications are stark. US & Canada generates **44.35% of Netflix's revenue with only ~30% of its subscribers**, while Asia-Pacific contributes just 11.4% of revenue despite holding 19% of subscribers. Netflix has consciously traded higher ARPU for subscriber growth in emerging markets — its mobile-only plans run 50–60% cheaper than basic plans in each country. Management describes these plans as "roughly revenue neutral" because lower ARPU is offset by reduced content delivery costs.

### VPN arbitrage exploits the price gap, but enforcement remains inconsistent

The 19x price differential creates strong incentives for VPN arbitrage, where users in expensive markets subscribe through cheaper countries using VPNs and regional gift cards, saving up to **75–80%** off US prices. Netflix combats this through IP address blacklisting, traffic pattern analysis, payment method restrictions, and household verification systems. The password-sharing crackdown infrastructure deployed in 2023 — which verifies household location through IP monitoring — has provided additional tools to detect VPN abuse. However, enforcement remains inconsistent: many users report years of successful VPN usage while others face account restrictions. Currency volatility adds another layer of complexity — Turkey's extreme inflation required multiple local price increases, while Argentina's peso depreciation forced Amazon to raise Prime Video prices from ARS 319 to ARS 1,149.

## How platforms decide what content goes where

Content acquisition decisions are driven by a framework balancing cost, engagement, and strategic value. Netflix tracks proprietary metrics including **cost per first stream** (what each new viewer costs to attract to a title), completion rates, subscriber attribution (which content drives sign-ups or retention), and what the company calls **"impact value"** — an internal metric estimating a title's total economic contribution. Bloomberg reported that Netflix's internal documents valued *Squid Game* Season 1 at **$891 million in impact value** against a production cost of just $21.4 million — a 42x return.

Content spending as a share of revenue has declined significantly as Netflix has scaled. In 2016, roughly **75% of every subscriber dollar** went to content. By 2024, Netflix spent approximately $17 billion on content against $39 billion in revenue — about **43%** — and the ratio continued improving toward 40% in 2025 with $18–18.6 billion projected against $44.8–45.2 billion in expected revenue. Globally, content cost per subscriber works out to approximately **$56/year or $4.70/month**, though this figure varies by region. A milestone was crossed in 2024 when **51% of Netflix's content budget** was spent outside North America for the first time, with Europe commanding roughly 35% of total spending.

Licensing deal durations follow predictable patterns: **first-look deals** run 2–5 years (Netflix paid a reported $250 million for a 5-year first-look deal with *Game of Thrones* creators David Benioff and D.B. Weiss), **output deals** span 4–7 years, **library deals** typically run 3–5 years, and **FAST/AVOD deals** increasingly feature shorter 1–3 year terms. When deals expire, content simply disappears from the platform — as millions of viewers discovered when *Friends* left Netflix for HBO Max and *The Office* migrated to Peacock. Netflix's 10-year exclusive holds on its earliest Originals are now beginning to expire, creating opportunities for studios to reclaim and relicense shows that Netflix funded.

## Case studies that reveal the real economics

### Squid Game: $21 million in, $891 million out

*Squid Game* Season 1 cost Netflix **$21.4 million total** — just $2.4 million per episode for nine episodes, cheaper than a single Dave Chappelle comedy special ($24.1 million). It became the most-watched Netflix content in history with 1.65 billion viewing hours in its first 28 days. Netflix's internal "impact value" metric pegged the show at **$891 million**, representing a roughly 42x return on investment. Season 2's budget reportedly jumped to **$84 million** (₩100 billion), reflecting both the franchise's proven value and rising Korean production costs. The show demonstrated Netflix's thesis that non-English originals produced at a fraction of Hollywood costs could generate outsized global engagement.

### The Crown: $504–$648 million across six seasons

Produced by Left Bank Pictures (acquired by Sony for £103 million in 2022) for Netflix under a cost-plus arrangement, *The Crown* cost approximately **$13–$14.4 million per episode**, totaling an estimated $504–$648 million across six seasons. Critically, Left Bank retained the underlying franchise rights — unusual for a Netflix production — because the deal was negotiated "at the beginning of Netflix's life" when producers had more leverage. Modern Netflix deals almost universally demand full global rights ownership. Left Bank's revenue reached £147.8 million ($177.5 million) in the year ending March 2022.

### A film's journey through the full windowing cycle

A 2025 wide-release film follows a compressed but still sequential path. Taking Disney's *Predator: Badlands* as a representative example: the film opened theatrically in November 2025 with a $105 million production budget, earned $162 million worldwide in its first three weeks (Disney retaining roughly 50% domestically, ~40–45% internationally), then moved to PVOD at approximately day 45–60 at $19.99 rental pricing (Disney retaining ~80%), before arriving on Disney+/Hulu approximately 80–100 days after theatrical. The economic logic is clear: a $20 PVOD rental where the studio keeps $16 can be more profitable per transaction than a $12 movie ticket where the studio keeps $6.

### FAST economics versus SVOD: different universes

The content cost gap between SVOD and FAST channels is enormous. While Netflix paid over **$500 million for Seinfeld's global streaming rights**, the same caliber of library content on FAST platforms might command **$500–$5,000 per title** as a flat fee, or modest recurring revenue through ad-revenue sharing. Fremantle extended its partnership with Pluto TV across 25 dedicated FAST channels in 13 international markets, licensing classic titles like *Baywatch* and *Three's Company*. FAST is designed as **incremental monetization** for libraries that would otherwise sit dormant — not a replacement for SVOD economics.

### ESPN's sports rights illustrate extreme territorial fragmentation

Disney/ESPN spends **$2.7 billion annually** on NFL Monday Night Football and **$2.6 billion/year** on NBA rights (part of the league's $76 billion, 11-year deal). NFL games now air across **10 different services**, costing consumers an estimated **$1,500/year** to access everything. ESPN launched ESPN Unlimited at $29.99/month and ESPN Select at $13/month in August 2025. The Premier League illustrates international fragmentation: UK rights are split between Sky Sports, TNT Sports, and Amazon Prime Video, while internationally the same competition is distributed across beIN (MENA), DAZN (Europe), JioStar (India), and Optus (Australia) — a single competition fragmented across a dozen platforms globally. The FCC launched a formal inquiry into sports media fragmentation in February 2026 after surveys showed **59% of sports fans** reporting difficulty finding or affording content.

## Conclusion: convergence is the next act

The streaming industry is entering a phase of structural consolidation that will reshape every dynamic covered in this guide. Territorial licensing is gradually giving way to global deals — Netflix's $7 billion+ Sony agreement through 2032 signals the direction — but pre-existing local contracts and regulatory frameworks like France's *chronologie des médias* will keep catalogs fragmented for years. The windowing system has compressed from 90-day theatrical exclusives to 30-day averages but is stabilizing as theaters push back, with multiple studios discussing a return to 45-day minimums.

The rebundling trend represents the industry's most significant structural shift since the launch of Disney+. With the Disney+/Hulu/Max bundle demonstrating 80% three-month retention and Charter's aggressive packaging slowing cord-cutting losses, the economic logic of bundling has been proven. The open question is whether price creep — averaging 12% annually — will eventually recreate the cable economics consumers fled. Regional pricing will continue reflecting massive purchasing-power disparities, with Netflix's 19x price spread between Pakistan and Switzerland likely widening as emerging-market competition from players like JioHotstar intensifies. The platforms that survive will be those that master three simultaneous challenges: acquiring content efficiently across territories, pricing it appropriately for radically different markets, and packaging it in bundles that reduce churn without triggering the subscription fatigue that already affects 62% of streaming households.
